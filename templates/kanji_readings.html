How kanji readings work

Most kanji have more than one reading, and no rule tells you for sure which one a word uses — in the end you learn it with the word. But a few patterns make a good guess much more likely.

{% set here = '/kanji/readings' %}{% include "_kanji_tabs.html" %}

Two kinds of reading

On'yomi (音読み, “sound reading”) came into Japanese with the kanji from Chinese. Dictionaries usually write it in katakana. Kun'yomi (訓読み) is a native Japanese word that was matched to the kanji's meaning; it's written in hiragana.

Every example on this page comes from the words course, and the readings are worked out from the dictionaries (JMdict and KANJIDIC2), not typed in by hand.

Sounds that change

Voicing (rendaku): the first sound of a later kanji often becomes voiced — か→が, さ→ざ, た→だ, は→ば.

So how do you get good at this?

  • Learn words, not reading lists. Each word you learn teaches you the reading a kanji really has there. Start with the words course.
  • Read a lot. The more words you've seen a kanji in, the better your guesses get for words you've never seen.
  • Practise guessing. Guess the reading shows you new words built from kanji you know.
  • When it matters, check. Names of people and places break the patterns most often. The dictionary is always one tap away.

Writing kanji by hand is a separate skill. If you want it, stroke order is on every kanji's page — but you don't need it to learn to read.
